For about a thousand years Sri Lanka's monks were split between rival fraternities that all traced their ordination to the same Theravāda Vinaya. The Mahāvihāra kept to the Pali canon and rejected the Mahāyāna scriptures. The Abhayagiri, and later the Jetavana, took in Mahāyāna and then Tantric teaching without leaving the Theravāda ordination line. Chinese pilgrims called such monks "Mahāyāna Sthaviras". This page sets out the evidence for that picture, how the Mahāyāna Theravādin line ended, how the Mahāvihāra tradition reached South-East Asia, and how two versions of the story that circulate among monastics compare with the sources.

## How this page uses "Theravāda"

A monastic school (nikāya) is named here by the Vinaya its monks are ordained under. "Theravāda" means the nikāya of the Theravāda (Pali) Vinaya, in the same way that Mūlasarvāstivāda, Sarvāstivāda, Dharmaguptaka and Mahāsāṃghika-Lokottaravāda name communities by their Vinayas. Two other questions are kept separate: which vehicle (yāna) a monk follows (Śrāvakayāna, Mahāyāna, Vajrayāna), and which tenet system they hold (Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra, Madhyamaka). A Tibetan monk is Mūlasarvāstivāda by Vinaya, Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna by vehicle, and usually Madhyamaka by tenet. "Theravāda" is not used as a synonym for Śrāvakayāna.

Two qualifications. First, the school names were doctrinal in origin: *vāda* means "doctrine", and Sarvāstivāda is "the doctrine that everything exists". Not every doctrinal school had a Vinaya of its own (the Sautrāntikas had none). Membership of a nikāya was nevertheless fixed by ordination, which is the sense used here. Second, the Mahāvihāra's own chronicle uses *thera* in a narrower sense. The Mahāvaṃsa calls the canon settled by Mahākassapa and the elders of the First Council "that of the theras", and treats every later school as a departure from it (Mhv 5.1–13). Geiger glosses *theravāda* there as "the true and orthodox church community" (Geiger 1912, p. 26 n.). When de Silva writes of "Theravāda orthodoxy", he means this Mahāvihāra sense.

The modern sense is different again. "Theravāda Buddhism" as the name of a whole religion, set against "Mahāyāna", is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century coinage, traced by Todd LeRoy Perreira (Perreira 2012).

## 1. What the Chinese pilgrims saw

### Yijing: the Vinaya does not change with the vehicle

Yijing (635–713) was a Vinaya specialist who studied at Nālandā and translated the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya into Chinese. In his *Record from the Southern Seas* he describes four main nikāyas and says monastics each keep to the rules of their own (出家之侶各依部執; T2125, T54: 205c2). He then separates the vehicle from the nikāya:

> 其四部之中，大乘小乘區分不定 … 考其致也，則律撿不殊，齊制五篇通修四諦，若禮菩薩、讀大乘經，名之為大；不行斯事，號之為小。(T2125, T54: 205c8–12)
>
> Within these four nikāyas the division into Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna is not fixed … Looking at what it comes to, their Vinaya does not differ: all regulate the five classes of offence and all practise the four truths. Those who venerate bodhisattvas and read Mahāyāna sūtras are called Mahāyāna; those who do not are called Hīnayāna. (Translated for this page.)

### Xuanzang: "Mahāyāna Sthaviras"

Xuanzang (c. 602–664) reports monks in several Indian regions who "study the Mahāyāna within the Sthavira school" (學大乘上座部法). Nearly a thousand were at Bodhgayā. For Sri Lanka, which he did not visit, he gives twenty thousand. His account contrasts the Mahāvihāra residents, who reject the Mahāyāna, with the Abhayagiri residents, who study both vehicles (*Da Tang xiyu ji*, T2087, as cited in Anālayo 2014 and the Encyclopedia of Buddhism, "Anurādhapura Mahāvihāra").

Max Deeg has suggested that the Sri Lankan figure may be Xuanzang raising Hīnayāna monks to a more respectable status for a Chinese readership. Anālayo thinks this possible for Sri Lanka but doubts it for the Indian cases (Anālayo 2014). Heinz Bechert read the phrase as this page does: the Mahāyāna Sthaviravādins still belonged to the Sthaviravāda "as far as bhikṣu ordination and vinaya-karma was concerned" (quoted in Anālayo 2014). Peter Skilling adds that "there is no such thing as a Mahāyāna bhikṣu ordination" (quoted in Anālayo 2014). An eleventh-century Khmer inscription of Sūryavarman I uses the same compound for monks ordained as *mahāyāna sthavira* bhikṣus (Anālayo 2014, following Bizot and Skilling).

### Daolin, Yijing and the fourth piṭaka

Yijing's biographies of pilgrim monks (T2066) show the layering inside a single career. Daolin spent three years at Tāmralipti learning Sanskrit, then "gave up his precepts and received them again" (捨戒重受) and studied the Sarvāstivāda (一切有部). Changing Vinaya lineage meant being ordained again. He also had a liking for the mantra collection. At Nālandā he read through Mahāyāna sūtras and śāstras and gave his attention to the Abhidharmakośa (T51: 6c15–18). Yijing then explains what the mantra collection is:

> 夫明呪者梵云毘睇陀羅必棏家 … 應云持明呪藏 … 梵本有十萬頌 (T51: 6c22–25)
>
> What is called "vidyā-mantra" is in Sanskrit *vidyādhara-piṭaka* … it should be rendered "piṭaka of the holders of vidyā-mantras" … the Sanskrit has a hundred thousand ślokas. (Translated for this page.)

Yijing also says that he himself "entered the maṇḍala several times" at Nālandā, hoping for this teaching, but could not pursue it alongside his other work (淨於那爛陀。亦屢入壇場希心此要。而為功不並就; T51: 7a11–12). The monk who carried the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya to China sought tantric initiation without any sense that it compromised his Vinaya.

## 2. The three fraternities

The Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura was the first foundation. The Abhayagiri was founded under Vaṭṭagāmaṇī Abhaya (c. 103 BCE), seceded from the Mahāvihāra and became a rival, independent fraternity (de Silva 1981, p. 47). King Mahāsena (274–301) founded the Jetavana, whose affiliated monasteries generally favoured the Abhayagiri (p. 48). The Mahāvaṃsa names the two breakaway groups as the Dhammaruci and the Sāgaliya (Mhv 5.13). For much of the Anurādhapura period the Abhayagiri had the larger following and the larger complex, and a reputation for learning abroad (de Silva 1981, p. 48).

Peter Skilling's assessment of what divided them is the most useful single statement (Skilling 1993, pp. 165–7):

- The two branches most probably shared the same Pali piṭakas, with relatively minor differences. Had the Abhayagiri adopted a Sanskrit Tipiṭaka, the Mahāvihāra would have said so, and it never does.
- The disputes the chronicles describe were usually connected in some way with the Vinaya. The Mahāvihāra's charge of Mahāyāna leanings "seems more of a blanket charge than the real cause of contention".
- Kings repeatedly tried to reunite the two, which would have been impossible had they held completely different Vinayas. The differences recorded in the subcommentaries matter only within a Theravādin frame.
- No undisputed Abhayagiri text survives. The Saddhammopāyana, the Questions of Upāli and the Vimuttimagga have been attributed to it, but each attribution is disputed. The Vimuttimagga survives in a sixth-century Chinese translation by *Saṅghabhara of Funan.

This refines the idea that Mahāyāna Theravādins exchanged their Sutta and Abhidhamma piṭakas for Mahāyāna ones. The better reading is that they kept the Pali piṭakas and added Mahāyāna and later Tantric texts on top, as Daolin added the Mahāyāna and the mantra collection to his Sarvāstivāda training.

## 3. Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna among the Sri Lankan Theravādins

Mahāyāna teaching reached Sri Lanka early. Āryadeva, Nāgārjuna's chief disciple, is believed to have come from the Sinhalese royal family (de Silva 1981, p. 47). The Mahāvihāra opposed the new teaching; the Abhayagiri received it. In the third century the Mahāvihāra persuaded Vohārika Tissa (209–31) to suppress it. Goṭhābhaya (249–62) could not be persuaded to use force and was somewhat sympathetic to it. Under Mahāsena the position was reversed (pp. 47–8; section 4 below).

De Silva sums up the long-run result: Mahāyāna "was never able to displace Theravada Buddhism from its position of primacy", but it changed Sri Lankan Buddhism profoundly (p. 49). The tooth relic arrived under Sirimeghavaṇṇa (301–28) under Mahāyāna auspices and was housed at the Abhayagiri, because the Mahāvihāra at first would have nothing to do with it. Its possession later became essential to kingship. Images of buddhas and bodhisattvas became a standard part of the monastery, and so did the belief that a righteous king could become a buddha in a future life (p. 49).

Tantric Buddhism followed. Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra found a large number of Tantric texts and ritual practices in Sri Lanka in the eighth century. In the ninth century two Tantric schools were introduced, the Nīlapaṭadarśana and the Vajravāda. The Vajravāda came under Sena I (833–53), brought by a monk from an Anurādhapura monastery, and Sena I became an adherent. Dhāraṇīs in the Indian nāgarī script have been found on stone, clay tablets and copper plaques across the old Rajarata, with bronze Tantric images such as Tārā (de Silva 1981, p. 51). Wikipedia's article on the Abhayagiri names the Tattvasaṃgraha as one tantra practised there, citing Chandawimala; this page has not checked that source. In Yijing's terms, part of the Sri Lankan saṅgha had added the vidyādhara-piṭaka to its Theravāda Vinaya and Pali piṭakas.

## 4. Mahāsena and the minister's army

The Mahāvaṃsa's account of Mahāsena's reign (Mhv 37.1–39, Geiger's translation) is the source of the story that an army forced a king to back down.

1. The Cola monk Saṅghamitta, who consecrated Mahāsena, told him that the Mahāvihāra residents "do not teach the (true) vinaya, we are those who teach the (true) vinaya". The king fined anyone who fed a Mahāvihāra monk a hundred pieces of money (vv. 1–5).
2. The monks left for Malaya, the central hills, and Rohaṇa in the south. The Mahāvihāra stood empty for nine years (vv. 6–7).
3. The minister Soṇa and other monks pulled down the seven-storey Lohapāsāda and carried the building materials to the Abhayagiri (vv. 10–12).
4. The minister Meghavaṇṇābhaya, "the friend of the king", rebelled in anger, raised "a great force" in Malaya and camped at the Dūratissaka tank (vv. 17–18). Geiger takes him to be marching to secure Rohaṇa (Geiger 1912, p. 268 n.).
5. The king marched out to fight, but no battle took place. Meghavaṇṇābhaya came to him alone at night with food and drink. They ate together, and the king promised to make the Mahāvihāra inhabited again and asked forgiveness (vv. 19–24).
6. One of the queens had Saṅghamitta killed, and Soṇa was killed as well. Meghavaṇṇābhaya rebuilt several parivenas and the monks returned (vv. 26–30).
7. Mahāsena then built the Jetavana inside the Mahāvihāra's boundaries. The monks refused to give up their boundary and abandoned the Mahāvihāra again, this time for nine months (vv. 32–37). The Jetavana's thera Tissa was charged with an offence "of the gravest kind" and expelled from the order by a minister, against the king's wishes (vv. 38–39).

Three points follow. The chronicle is partisan: it calls the Mahāvihāra "our Mahāvihāra" (v. 7). As it tells the story, the quarrel was over the Vinaya, which fits Skilling's reading. And the episode did not end with Mahāyāna leaving the island. De Silva notes that the Mahāvihāra's ties with the population, above all the nobility, restrained Mahāsena, and that the Mahāvihāra recovered much of its position under his successor (de Silva 1981, p. 48). The Abhayagiri and the new Jetavana went on to flourish for another eight centuries.

## 5. How the Mahāyāna Theravādin line ended

The decisive damage came from the Cola conquest and the period of Cola rule that followed. Property losses under Cola rule, and again in the interregnum between Vijayabāhu I and Parākramabāhu I, harmed all three nikāyas alike. They broke up and regrouped as eight fraternities (de Silva 1981, p. 73). Vijayabāhu I (1055–1110) turned to Burma for monks to restore the saṅgha (pp. 51, 66).

Parākramabāhu I (1153–86) then brought the eight fraternities under a common leadership. The sources disagree on what this meant.

- **The traditional reading.** The Mahāvihāra triumphed and the Abhayagiri and Jetavana were suppressed. Wikipedia's article on the Abhayagiri cites the Cūḷavaṃsa (78.1–27) for the monks of the two being defrocked. It also cites Richard Gombrich, who writes that the king laicised many Mahāvihāra monks and all the monks of the other two nikāyas, letting the better ones become novices to be re-ordained in due course (Gombrich 1988, p. 159). This page has not checked either source directly.
- **De Silva, following R. A. L. H. Gunawardana's *Robe and Plough*.** "Recent research has shown this to be quite inaccurate" (de Silva 1981, p. 73). On this reading the unification was much more, and much less, than imposing the Mahāvihāra's authority on the other two, and it did not end sectarian competition.

Both readings agree on the outcome. The ordination line and texts that survived are the Mahāvihāra's. In Skilling's words, the Mahāvihāra "won out" and the Abhayagiri disappeared; the Theravāda known today is the Mahāvihāra tradition as settled mainly by Buddhaghosa (Skilling 1993, p. 165). The Mahāyāna did not vanish without trace, however. By the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries the conflict had been settled by absorbing some Mahāyāna doctrines, rituals and deities into the official religion, which by then was no longer purely Theravāda in the Mahāvihāra sense (de Silva 1981, pp. 92–3).

## 6. Decline and the Siamese restoration

By the eighteenth century the Sri Lankan saṅgha had declined to the point where a valid higher ordination (upasampadā) could not be held. Earlier missions to Arakan in Burma under Vimala Dharma Sūriya I and II brought only temporary relief. Most of those entering the order were *gaṇinnānses*, who kept their lay names, wore white or saffron cloth and rarely lived celibate lives (de Silva 1981, p. 201).

Reform came from inside the saṅgha. The novice Väliviṭa Saraṇaṅkara (1698–1778) founded the Silvat Samāgama. The Nāyakkar kings were willing to act on its programme. Three missions went to Thailand with Dutch ships; those of 1741 and 1747 failed, and the third left in 1750 and returned in 1753 (pp. 154–5, 202). In 1753 Thai monks restored the upasampadā in Sri Lanka, and the Siyam Nikāya was founded. In 1765 Kīrti Śrī Rājasiṃha restricted higher ordination to the Malvatta and Asgiriya chapters, and confined the Siyam Nikāya to the goyigama caste. That restriction led, around the turn of the nineteenth century, to the founding of the Amarapura Nikāya in the low country (pp. 202–4). The Thai monks also reordered the Kandy Äsala perahära so that the tooth-relic procession took first place over the Hindu deities' processions (p. 202).

The direction matters for the popular versions discussed below. In the eighteenth century Sri Lanka took its ordination from Thailand, not the reverse.

## 7. The spread to South-East Asia

Sinhalese Theravāda spread through South-East Asia from the Polonnaruva period onwards (de Silva 1981, p. 74). Once Buddhism had disappeared from India, Burma, Thailand and Cambodia treated Sri Lanka as a surrogate holy land because of the relics kept there (p. 93).

The movement was carried by Vinaya lineage. A. B. Griswold and Prasert na Nagara describe three orders of monks at Chiang Mai and Keng Tung (Griswold and Prasert 1978, pp. 66–7):

- An older order of town- and village-dwelling monks, probably descended from the order at Haripuñjaya before the Tai conquest.
- The "Old Ceylon Sect" of forest dwellers. It followed the Vinaya as interpreted by the Mahāvihāra, in particular its forest-dwelling branch at Udumbaragiri in Polonnaruva. It was established at Martaban in Lower Burma in the fourteenth century, favoured at Sukhothai, and brought to Lan Na by the Mahāthera Sumana in 1370.
- The "New Ceylon Sect". Around 1420 several Chiang Mai monks began to doubt that they had been validly ordained through an unbroken succession. In 1423, "in order to satisfy their doubts", twenty-five mahātheras from Chiang Mai, with eight from Ayutthaya, went to the Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura. Finding flaws in their own ordinations, they disrobed and were re-ordained. They returned to Chiang Mai in 1430, founded the New Ceylon Sect at the Red Forest Monastery, and persuaded many local monks to re-ordain into it. King Sam Fang Kaen, fearing a schism, expelled them from the capital; his son Tilokarāja, who deposed him in 1441, favoured them.

A British Library blog post gives the companions of 1423 as eight monks from Angkor rather than Ayutthaya, and adds that in 1476 twenty-two Burmese monks were ordained in Sri Lanka, after which King Dhammaceti built the Kalyāṇī Sīmā at Pegu (San San May and Igunma, n.d.).

### Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna in Thailand

Before the Sinhalese lineage arrived, Mahāyāna was practised in the Srivijaya-influenced south and in the Khmer-ruled centre around Lopburi. Ram Khamhaeng of Sukhothai is credited with supporting Lankavong monks from Nakhon Si Thammarat, although the traditional account of his adopting Theravāda as the state religion is disputed. At first both Theravāda and Mahāyāna were practised at Sukhothai, and Ayutthaya kept Mahāyāna minorities (Wikipedia, "Buddhism in Thailand"; "Ramkhamhaeng").

Vajrayāna is attested too. The Sab Bak inscription from central Siam is evidence of an early Vajrayāna presence. It mentions an "Abhaya mountain", though it is unclear whether this has anything to do with Sri Lanka's Abhayagiri (Chirapat 1990, as cited in Skilling 1993, pp. 166–7 n.).

Mahāyāna never disappeared from Thailand. Chinese (Chin Nikāya) and Vietnamese (Anam Nikāya) Mahāyāna orders still operate there, and the Anam Nikāya has an administrative structure under the Thai Sangha Acts (Buddhisaro et al. 2021; Wu 2017). The picture is one of gradual change through royal patronage of the Sinhalese lineage. No source consulted for this page records a king eliminating Mahāyāna or Vajrayāna.

## 8. Two popular versions compared with the sources

Two versions of this history circulated in a conversation among monastics in October 2026. Version A held that Sri Lankan Buddhism had been Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna; that war emptied every monastery except the Mahāvihāra; that a king then made the Mahāvihāra's Pali tradition the main religion; and that Thailand, having lost its monks to war, took ordination from Sri Lanka, so that all modern Theravāda comes from one monastery and the Mahāyāna Theravāda tradition is lost. Version B held that a king wanted Mahāyāna as the sole state religion, that Theravāda forces raised an army in the south and forced him to recognise Theravāda instead, and that Mahāyāna was expelled; and that a Thai king chose Theravāda alone and eliminated Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna.

| Claim | Version | Verdict | Basis |

| Sri Lankan Buddhism was originally Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna | A | Wrong | The Mahāvihāra came first; the Abhayagiri seceded from it. Mahāyāna arrived later and Tantra later still (§§2–3) |
| War emptied every monastery except the Mahāvihāra | A | Wrong | The Cola conquest harmed all three nikāyas, which regrouped as eight fraternities (§5) |
| A king then made the Mahāvihāra tradition dominant | A | Partly right | Parākramabāhu I unified the saṅgha in the twelfth century; how far this was a Mahāvihāra victory is disputed (§5) |

| Thailand lost its monks to war and took ordination from Sri Lanka | A | Direction reversed | Thai monks did seek re-ordination in Sri Lanka (1423), but because they doubted their ordination's validity. In 1753 Thai monks restored Sri Lanka's ordination (§§6–7) |

## Open questions

- **What the Abhayagiri's Vinaya differences were.** Skilling says the subcommentaries record points of difference, but no Abhayagiri text survives to give its side.
- **How Parākramabāhu I treated the Abhayagiri and Jetavana monks.** The Cūḷavaṃsa and Gombrich (defrocking and re-ordination) need to be read against Gunawardana's *Robe and Plough*. None of the three has been consulted directly for this page.
- **Xuanzang's Sri Lankan figures** are second-hand. Deeg's suspicion of an upgrade for Chinese readers has not been settled.
- **The 1423 mission.** Griswold and Prasert have eight companions from Ayutthaya; the British Library post has eight from Angkor.
- **The Sab Bak "Abhaya mountain".** Whether it connects central Siamese Vajrayāna with the Abhayagiri is unknown.
- **The Thai suppression story.** If a source for a king eliminating Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna turns up, the verdict in §8 should be revisited.
