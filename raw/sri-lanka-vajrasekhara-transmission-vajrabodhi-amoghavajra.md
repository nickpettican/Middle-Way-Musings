# Sri Lanka and the Transmission of the *Vajraśekhara* to China: Vajrabodhi and Amoghavajra

*Research note compiled by Claude (Opus 5.5) on 28 October 2026 at Tenpa's request, from the sources listed at the end. Chinese primary passages were read from the CBETA edition of the Taishō canon. Claims taken from a secondary source are cited to that source; inferences of my own are marked as such.*

## The question

Vajrabodhi (671–741) and his disciple Amoghavajra (705–774) are credited with establishing esoteric Buddhism at the Tang court. Through Amoghavajra's student Huiguo 惠果 it reached Kūkai in 805 and became the Shingon school in Japan. Both men are said to have visited Sri Lanka. Did they receive their tantras there, and if so, which ones?

## Vajrabodhi: a pilgrim, not a recipient

The earliest account of Vajrabodhi is the biography by his lay disciple Lü Xiang 呂向, written around 760 and preserved in Yuanzhao's catalogue of 800 (T 2157, 55.875a–876b; translated in Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 134–139). Lü Xiang says Vajrabodhi studied at Nālandā and, at thirty-one, went to South India, where he served Nāgajñāna 龍智 for seven years. From him he received the *Jingangding yuqie jing* 金剛頂瑜伽經 (presumably a version of the *Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha*, hereafter STTS), Vairocana's dhāraṇī teachings, and initiation into the five families.

Only after this did he go to Siṃhala. He spent half a year worshipping the Tooth Relic at the Abhayagiri ("Abhayarāja Monastery"), climbed Adam's Peak to venerate the footprint, and taught Mahāyāna to the Theravādin king of Rohaṇa. After about a year he went back to South India. He then sailed from Sri Lanka by way of Śrīvijaya and reached Guangzhou in 719 or 720. In this account the Sri Lankan visit is a pilgrimage. The tantric training had already taken place on the mainland, in the Pallava domain centred on Kāñcī (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 145–146).

Two qualifications apply. A preface probably written by Vajrabodhi's Korean collaborator Hyech'o 慧超 (T 1077) says that in 741 Vajrabodhi returned an esoteric manuscript to his master in Siṃhala, an ācārya *Ratnabodhi. The same preface contains factual errors, so its weight is limited (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 192–193 n. 97). Separately, K. M. de Silva's general history says that both monks collected "a large number of Tantric texts" on the island (de Silva 1981, 51). For Vajrabodhi, Lü Xiang does not bear this out.

## Amoghavajra: a documented Sri Lankan transmission

Vajrabodhi lost the full "100,000-verse" text of his central scripture overboard in a typhoon on the voyage to China and kept only an abridged version. Amoghavajra later retold the story in Vajrabodhi's words (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 147). Vajrabodhi's 723 rendering of the first part of the STTS (T 866) differs so much from the Sanskrit, the Tibetan and later Chinese versions that it is usually treated as a ritual manual, perhaps rebuilt from memory.

After Vajrabodhi's death in 741, Amoghavajra went abroad to find the complete texts. Three Tang-period sources describe the journey.

- **Feixi 飛錫**, Amoghavajra's disciple and translation assistant, wrote the stele inscription commissioned on Amoghavajra's death in 774. It says the emperor sent Amoghavajra to Siṃhala as an envoy and that the king received him with great honour. There an ācārya named Samantabhadra 普賢 conferred on him the eighteen-assembly *Vajraśekhara* yoga (十八會金剛頂瑜伽), the "Great Compassion Matrix" of Vairocana (毘盧遮那大悲胎藏), the five-family abhiṣeka and more than 500 Sanskrit texts. He returned "from Siṃhala" in 747 (T 2120, 52.848b–c).
- **Zhao Qian 趙遷**'s account of Amoghavajra's conduct names the same teacher and the same teachings. It adds that the voyage fulfilled Vajrabodhi's dying instruction to go to Siṃhala, and dates the return to 746. Amoghavajra brought back a memorial from King Śīlamegha 尸羅迷伽 (Aggabodhi VI, r. 733–772) and a Sanskrit Prajñāpāramitā (T 2056, 50.292c–293a).
- **Amoghavajra's own testament** says that from Vajrabodhi he had received only a "4,000-verse" yoga text, and that abroad he obtained the "100,000-verse" corpus (T 2120, 52.844a).

The sources disagree on two points. First, Zhao Qian inserts a vague line that Amoghavajra "also travelled the Five Indias", and Amoghavajra himself speaks of going to 天竺 (India), while Feixi names only Siṃhala. Sundberg argues that the journey went no further than Sri Lanka (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 148–151). Hyech'o's preface places Siṃhala "in South India among the Five Indias", so the Chinese term may have been loose enough to cover the island. That last point is my inference, not Sundberg's. Second, from 781 onwards (Yan Ying 嚴郢, T 2120, 52.847a, 860b) and in Kūkai's lineage writings, the Sri Lankan teacher is named as Nāgajñāna rather than Samantabhadra. Following Matsunaga, Sundberg reads this as a way of giving Amoghavajra a direct line to Nāgajñāna (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 193–194 n. 98).

## Which texts

No source itemises the 500 manuscripts, so the link between a given translation and the Sri Lankan collection is sometimes inferred. The table grades each case.

| Text | Translator and date | Sri Lankan link |
|---|---|---|
| STTS, first part (金剛頂瑜伽真實大教王經, T 865, 3 fasc.) | Amoghavajra; first title on his list of translations submitted in 771 (T 2120, 52.839a) | Strong. It agrees with the Sanskrit and Tibetan versions where Vajrabodhi's does not, and was "seemingly gathered from his Śrī Laṅkān expedition" (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 147) |
| Outline of the eighteen assemblies (金剛頂瑜伽十八會指歸, T 869) | Amoghavajra | Strong. This is the corpus that Feixi and Zhao Qian say he received in Siṃhala. Sundberg notes that it includes assemblies corresponding to the *Guhyasamāja* and *Sarvabuddhasamāyoga* (Sundberg 2014, 108) |
| *Prajñāpāramitā-naya* (金剛頂瑜伽般若理趣經, generally identified with T 243) | Amoghavajra; second on the 771 list | Probable. It belongs to the *Vajraśekhara* cycle, but no source ties this text individually to Sri Lanka |
| *Sarvatathāgatādhiṣṭhāna-hṛdaya-guhyadhātu* dhāraṇī (寶篋經, T 1022) | Amoghavajra; also among the Siddham manuscripts Kūkai took to Japan | Physical evidence. The text is inscribed on the dhāraṇī stones of the Abhayagiri (Schopen 1982 and Giebel 2012, both cited in Sundberg 2014, 73 n. 41) |
| *Mahāvairocana-sūtra* (T 848) | Śubhakarasiṃha with Yixing, 724–725, from a manuscript collected in India by Wuxing 無行 (d. c. 674) | Not from Sri Lanka as a text. Amoghavajra received the Garbha teaching there, but Shingon uses this earlier translation |

## Were these texts current in Sri Lanka?

If Sri Lanka was where Amoghavajra found the full Yoga-tantra library, one would expect traces of it on the island. They exist, though most are from the eighth and ninth centuries.

- The dhāraṇī stones from the Abhayagiri combine the Mahāyāna dhāraṇī above with dhāraṇī elements from a subsidiary maṇḍala of the STTS (Chandawimala 2013, cited in Sundberg 2014, 73).
- The double-platform monastery at Tiriyāy held the largest cache of esoteric and Mahāyāna statues yet found on the island (Sundberg 2014, 49–50).
- De Silva records ninth-century dhāraṇīs in Nāgarī script on stone, clay tablets and copper plaques across Rajarata, bronze images of Tārā, and a Vajravāda sect introduced in the reign of Sena I (833–853) (de Silva 1981, 51).
- The kings who received the two monks, Mānavarman (r. 684–718) and Aggabodhi VI, were sympathetic to Mahāyāna. Aggabodhi VI sent a Prajñāpāramitā to the Tang court with Amoghavajra (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 145–146, 186 n. 72).
- In 792 the Śailendra kings of Java invited monks from the Abhayagiri to settle at Ratu Baka. Sundberg reads this as the Javanese "tracking back Amoghavajra's sources in Śrī Laṅkā" (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 131).

This supports Sri Lanka as the place where the *Vajraśekhara* corpus was available in the 740s. It does not show that the texts were composed there. Weinberger dates the STTS to late seventh-century India (Weinberger 2003, cited on Wikipedia), and Vajrabodhi first met it in Pallava South India.

## Assessment

- **Well attested.** Amoghavajra obtained the eighteen-assembly *Vajraśekhara* corpus (with the complete STTS) and a second abhiṣeka in Sri Lanka in 742–746/747. Feixi's stele, written days after his death, says so directly.
- **Not supported.** Vajrabodhi received his tantras in Sri Lanka. Lü Xiang places his training in South India and his Sri Lankan stay as a pilgrimage.
- **Hypothesis.** Sundberg goes further. He suggests that the ascetic pāṃśukūlika monks of the Abhayagiri were the specific source of Amoghavajra's texts, that Sri Lankan monks may have helped compose them, and that Nāgajñāna himself may have moved to the island (Sundberg and Giebel 2011, 150–151; Sundberg 2014, 108). He offers these as plausible narratives, not established findings.

The Shingon line therefore runs from Samantabhadra in Sri Lanka to Amoghavajra, Huiguo and Kūkai, but only on the Vajradhātu side. The Garbhadhātu side rests on the *Mahāvairocana-sūtra* as translated by Śubhakarasiṃha, who came to China overland from Nālandā.
