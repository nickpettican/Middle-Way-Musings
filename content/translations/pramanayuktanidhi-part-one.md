## Foreword

The *Treasury of Reasoning on Valid Cognition* (ཚད་མ་རིགས་པའི་གཏེར་, *Pramāṇayuktanidhi*) is Sakya Paṇḍita's (ས་སྐྱ་པཎྜི་ཏ་) verse treatise on the system of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. It has eleven chapters. In the commentarial outline they fall into two parts. Chapters 1 to 7 establish the knowable from its general aspects (ཤེས་བྱ་སྤྱི་ལྡོག་ནས་གཏན་ལ་དབབ་པ་), and chapters 8 to 11 ascertain the nature of the valid cognitions that cognise it (ཤེས་བྱེད་ཚད་མའི་རང་བཞིན་ངེས་པར་བྱེད་པ་). This page gives the first part; chapters 1 to 3 are translated so far.

The meaning follows Tenzin Jamtsho's translation in *Sa-paṇ's Dialectics* (2026). The Tibetan is the Sakya Kabum text (ས་སྐྱ་བཀའ་འབུམ་, vol. ད); where it differs from Jamtsho's critical edition, the notes say which reading the translation follows. The notes draw mainly on Sakya Paṇḍita's auto-commentary, on Khenpo Sodargye's lectures and on Jamtsho, with the later Tibetan commentators (Gorampa, Śākya Chokden, Rongtön, Lowo Khenchen and others) cited by name.

Rivals' words are in double quotes, and everything outside the quotes is Sakya Paṇḍita's. Who is speaking is added in square brackets to the root's own "if you say" (or "you may say", where the answer refutes the claim) or "so they say".

This page gives the English only. For the Tibetan and English verse by verse, see the [Pramāṇayuktanidhi in the Pramāṇa Wiki](https://pramana.middlewaymusings.com/texts/pramanayuktanidhi/).

## Title and Homage

In Sanskrit: *Pramāṇayuktanidhi*. In Tibetan: *Treasury of Reasoning on Valid Cognition*.[^c1-1]

With respect I prostrate to the Ārya Mañjuśrī Kumārabhūta.

With vast eyes that see all objects of knowledge,  
with compassion that accomplishes the welfare of every being,  
and with the power to perform inconceivable enlightened activity:  
at the feet of the Guru Mañjunātha I bow my head.[^c1-2]

The crown of your intellect adorned with the jewel ornaments of good qualities,  
your tongue uttering the two truths resounding the *phuṭ* of emptiness;  
your gaze of wisdom and compassion, like light rays, unbearable to opposing disputants:  
glorious Nāga Lord, ornament of the ocean of the clear-minded, be victorious![^c1-3]

Here among the snow mountains, hundreds of millions of star-like scholars  
have opened the lotus-like scriptures of the glorious [Dignāga and] Dharmakīrti;  
yet until the sunlight of my understanding spread over it,  
they could not bring to light its heart, the stamens:  
the intended meaning of the treatises.[^c1-4]

Having seen well, with the eye of clear intelligence,  
the system of Dharmakīrti just as he held it,  
I shall explain this, with a loving mind, for others:  
the intelligent, the impartial, and those who strive for the meaning.

## Chapter One: Examining Objects

*Tibetan and English: [chapter 1 in the Pramāṇa Wiki](https://pramana.middlewaymusings.com/texts/pramanayuktanidhi/chapter-1-objects).*

The definition of an object is: what is cognisable by the mind.[^c1-5]

If you [early Tibetan logicians][^c1-6] say,  
"The two, the conceptual generality[^c1-7]  
and the non-existent appearance, are objects",  
then it is contradictory for the two cognitions  
that apprehend them to be mistaken.

While you [Tibetan logicians] say, "Even when the object exists,  
[a cognition] can be mistaken,  
like the apprehension of a rope as a snake",  
[actually] the apprehension of the mottled [rope]  
is non-mistaken, since its object exists,  
and the apprehension of a snake is mistaken  
because its object does not exist.

If the two appearing objects  
were different from the cognitions,  
any other person standing within range  
would also see them, as with a vase and the like.

You [Tibetan logicians] may say,  
"Though they are objects, the two mistaken appearances  
are like the inside of the body, which is not seen:  
because they are always related to oneself,  
others do not realise them".

[However] the inside of the body is not a viable object,  
and that is why even oneself does not see it.  
If you say, "Since they are always related to oneself,  
the two are not viable objects either",  
then, being related to one's own mind alone,  
they will not be realised by others even when expressed.

While you [Tibetan logicians] may say,  
"Since each is related to its own mind,  
that very thing is realised through expression",  
they cannot be linked,  
since the two persons' objects of expression are separate.

You [Tibetan logicians] may say,  
"The two mental aspects in the separate persons are similar,  
and convention is applied to what is similar,  
conceived as one; so there is no contradiction".

[However] if it appears to oneself, it is one's own object;  
if it does not appear, it is not one's object.  
So how could things apprehended as different  
possibly be apprehended as one?

If you [Tibetan logicians] say, "Although they are different,  
they are apprehended as one object by way of mistake",  
then, since apprehending them as one is mistaken,  
it is established that there is no apprehended object.

Although they do not exist when examined by reasoning,  
most still conceive of them as objects,  
as when someone points with a fingertip  
and a fool, deluded, thinks they see the sky.  
*This is an interstitial verse.*

The object of comprehension  
is the specifically characterised phenomenon[^c1-8] alone.

If you say, "This is undermined  
by [Dignāga's] assertion of two objects":  
specifically and generally characterised phenomena  
are two by way of the mode of engagement.[^c1-9]

You may say, "Then the non-existent  
would equally be an object of comprehension";  
[however,] it has neither the purpose nor the ability of an object.  
The appearance of [floating] hairs and the like is mind itself;  
what appears as hair is not established in reality.  
When conceptual cognition examines  
whether it exists as an entity or not,  
it is a generally characterised phenomenon[^c1-10].

You [Tibetan logicians][^c1-11] may say,  
"The two non-entities are established implicitly  
by self-awareness, so [your position] is overturned";  
[however,] the two mistaken cognitions have no object,  
and since they are experienced by self-awareness,  
they are cognition itself;  
and since they apprehend the non-existent,  
they are mistaken cognitions.

If you say, "If there is no object  
other than the specifically characterised,  
it is contradictory for non-entities to be objects of comprehension",  
there is no contradiction, since [a non-entity] is established  
[by asking] whether the object of negation exists as an entity or not.

"The learned have refuted the external apprehended object,  
and an apprehended object that is cognition is not possible here;  
so not even the one object of comprehension is tenable", some declare.[^c1-12]

That [the cause of things] is intrinsic nature, Īśvara,  
primal substance, atoms, cognisance or dependent arising:  
these are the different tenets held by schools of our own and others.[^c1-13]

"Since what is obstructed is not [seen],  
it is not consciousness [that sees];  
the sense faculty serving as support is the seer",  
so [the Vaibhāṣikas][^c1-14] say.  
[However,] being matter, it is not a seer;  
and between simultaneous things there is no relationship.

"Object and consciousness, being simultaneous,  
are apprehended and apprehender",  
say those of the Land of Snow.[^c1-15]  
Being simultaneous, they have no relationship;  
and consciousness without a cause opposes [reason].  
You may say, "The object of the previous moment is the cause,  
and the simultaneous one is the apprehended object",  
[yet] consciousness is established because it arose from the object,  
and for that nothing simultaneous is needed.

That which arises from object, sense faculty  
and mental engagement[^c1-16] is consciousness.[^c1-17]  
Although food and the like are causes of a child,  
it is born resembling its parents' form;  
[so too consciousness] arises resembling its object.  
Hence the object has two results,  
and the mind too is held to have two aspects.

Being neither one nor many,  
[external] objects do not exist,  
and so neither do [their] aspects.[^c1-18]

If, for the relative, one relies on the common renown of the world,  
the system of valid cognition falls into contradiction.[^c1-19]

[That all knowables are mind] is established  
by clarity and by co-apprehension[^c1-20].  
For what is co-apprehended, being other is impossible;  
were they different substances, the reason would be impossible.  
As with the appearance of two moons,  
so too with blue and its apprehender.  
[The reason] is excluded from what is other,  
since the alternatives are negated.[^c1-21]

"The mental continuum is like a whole[^c1-22],  
and its moments are like the parts of an atom;  
so, since [a moment] has the nature of three moments,  
it is neither one nor many", so [the Mādhyamikas][^c1-23] say.  
If it has the nature of three moments,  
a single moment becomes impossible;  
and if a single moment is impossible,  
having the nature of three is lost.

In a coarse [object], because [the atoms] surround it all at once,  
the central atom comes to have parts;  
but since the three times do not arise all at once,  
the present moment is partless.

What appears as an object is mind itself;  
that very appearance does not exist outside.  
True and false are presented  
on the basis of stable and unstable traces[^c1-24].[^c1-25]

So long as external [objects] are accepted,  
the cause is called the apprehended object;  
once objects of knowledge are drawn inward,  
object and subject are not established.  
*This is a summary verse.*[^c1-26]

From the *Treasury of Reasoning on Valid Cognition*, the first chapter: the examination of objects.

[^c1-1]: Jamtsho's critical edition gives the Sanskrit as *Pramāṇayuktinidhi*; the Tibetan text's transliteration of the Sanskrit title reads *-yukta-*.

[^c1-2]: The auto-commentary does not comment on the four opening verses. The exegetical tradition takes the guru to be Sakya Paṇḍita's uncle and teacher Drakpa Gyaltsen (གྲགས་པ་རྒྱལ་མཚན་), and Sangye Pal's commentary says so explicitly.

[^c1-3]: Jamtsho reads this as homage to Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, the founders of Buddhist epistemology, praised through the figure of the Nāga king: his jewelled crown is their intellect set with qualities, the *phuṭ* of his tongue their teaching of the two truths and emptiness, his unbearable gaze their wisdom and compassion, and his place as ornament of the ocean their place among the learned.

[^c1-4]: དཔལ་ལྡན་གྲགས་པ་: Jamtsho reads "the Glorious One [Dignāga] and Dharmakīrti". Sakya Paṇḍita names none of the star-like scholars. Ngawang Chodrak identifies them as Ngok Lotsawa, Cha-ba Chökyi Sengge and Tsangnakpa Tsöndrü Sengge; Uyukpa Rigpé Sengge, the earliest commentator, as the early translators Kawa Paltsek and Chokro Lü'i Gyaltsen.

[^c1-5]: Rongtön Sheja Kunrig reads བློ་ here as *valid* cognition: whatever is an object is established by valid cognition.

[^c1-6]: The opponents in this exchange are the early Tibetan logicians, notably Cha-ba, who held the conceptual generality and the non-existent appearance (hairs seen with an eye disease, a double moon) to be real apprehended objects (Sodargye). Sakya Paṇḍita attributes a similar view to the Hearer (Śrāvaka) schools, and Gorampa traces the Tibetan idea back to Dharmottara. Jamtsho thinks the arguments are most likely aimed at Tsurtön Zhönnu Sengge, whose commentary carries the same threefold list.

[^c1-7]: Sanskrit *arthasāmānya*.

[^c1-8]: Sanskrit: object of comprehension, *prameya*; specifically characterised phenomenon, *svalakṣaṇa*.

[^c1-9]: Dignāga's twofold division is made by the mode of apprehension: non-conceptual cognition apprehends only the specifically characterised phenomenon, conceptual cognition the generally characterised (Sodargye).

[^c1-10]: Sanskrit *sāmānyalakṣaṇa*.

[^c1-11]: Sodargye identifies these objectors as the early Tibetan logicians.

[^c1-12]: The commentaries do not name these scholars. The objection runs two systems together: the apprehended object belongs to the Sautrāntika account, where external objects are granted, and its denial to the Chittamatra account. The summary verse that closes the chapter gives the answer.

[^c1-13]: Sodargye identifies the schools: intrinsic nature, the Charvakas; Īśvara, the followers of Maheśvara; primal substance, the Sāṃkhyas; atoms, the Vaiśeṣikas and the Buddhist Vaibhāṣikas and Sautrāntikas; cognisance, Chittamatra; dependent arising, Madhyamaka.

[^c1-14]: Their argument: consciousness is immaterial and so cannot be obstructed; if it were the seer, one would see through walls. Since obstruction blocks sight, the material faculty must be what sees (Sodargye). Jamtsho traces the view to *Abhidharmakośa* I.42ab. In the auto-commentary's outline, this verse and the next refute other views before the Sautrāntika position is stated.

[^c1-15]: The early Tibetan logicians. Sodargye notes that they drop the Vaibhāṣika claim that the faculty sees but keep the simultaneity of object and cognition. Jamtsho names Cha-ba, for whom subject and object must be simultaneous if cognition is to reach its object at all.

[^c1-16]: Sanskrit *manaskāra*.

[^c1-17]: Sakya Paṇḍita here states his own position within the Sautrāntika system, which he calls the system of the object knower (དོན་རིག་). Sodargye explains the object's "two results" as the continuation of its own kind and the cognition that bears its form. The mind's "two aspects" are, in the auto-commentary, the cognition that knows the object and the self-awareness that knows that cognition; Gorampa takes them as the objective aspect and the subjective aspect.

[^c1-18]: With this verse Sakya Paṇḍita moves from the Sautrāntika to the Chittamatra system. In the *Entrance Gate for the Wise* (མཁས་པ་རྣམས་འཇུག་པའི་སྒོ་) he warns that reading "neither one nor many" here as a Madhyamaka argument mistakes the context: here it is a Chittamatra reasoning.

[^c1-19]: Neither Sakya Paṇḍita nor his commentators name the target. Sodargye describes early logicians who proposed settling conventions in Candrakīrti's Prāsaṅgika way, accepting what the world accepts without analysis. Jamtsho points to Cha-ba's remark that double moons and conceptual generalities "abide as they are renowned in the world" as the kind of view under attack.

[^c1-20]: Sanskrit *sahopalambha*.

[^c1-21]: The auto-commentary gives two separate proofs that all knowables are mind. **Clarity:** the defining characteristic of cognition is to be clear and aware, and nothing knowable lacks it, so everything knowable is established as mind, just as a hump and dewlap establish a cow. **Co-apprehension:** whatever is invariably apprehended together (ལྷན་ཅིག་དམིགས་ངེས་; the root drops ངེས་) is not different, like the appearance of two moons, and blue and the cognition that apprehends it are invariably apprehended together. སྔོ་འཛིན་ is therefore the pair "blue and its apprehender", not "the apprehension of blue". The auto-commentary calls this an observation of something contradictory to the pervader: being different is pervaded by being different substances at different times, and invariable co-apprehension contradicts that. The last line gives the negative pervasion (ལྡོག་ཁྱབ་), that co-apprehension never occurs in the dissimilar class (མི་མཐུན་ཕྱོགས་) of different things. The auto-commentary takes the alternatives in turn. Different things at different times are apprehended at different times, so they are not co-apprehended. A superimposition at the same time is known to be a superimposition, so its object is nothing other than the cognition. Different substances at the same time are ruled out by an invalidating reason (གནོད་པ་ཅན་གྱི་རྟགས་): what is unrelated is not invariably apprehended together, like blue and yellow. Jamyang Loter Wangpo's commentary (tr. Roger Espel Llima) reads the last line differently, as a quoted counterargument, "different [things] have different scopes", which refutes being substantially different on two grounds: different entities are apprehended separately and at different times, and an invalidating reason applies to simultaneous ones. The substance matches the auto-commentary; the two part over whether མཐའ་གཞན་ means "the alternatives" or "different scopes".

[^c1-22]: Sanskrit *avayavin*.

[^c1-23]: The Mādhyamika turns the "neither one nor many" reasoning back on the idealist's partless moment of cognition. Sodargye notes that Sakya Paṇḍita answers while provisionally standing within the Chittamatra position. The next verse completes the reply: atoms are surrounded by other atoms at the same time, but the three times never arise together.

[^c1-24]: Sanskrit *vāsanā*.

[^c1-25]: Sakya Paṇḍita's answer to how idealism can tell true from false without external objects: what most beings perceive through stable, enduring traces counts conventionally as correct, and what is perceived through unstable, temporary traces as incorrect, as with one moon and two (Sodargye).

[^c1-26]: This answers the objection "some declare" above. As Gorampa's verse closing the chapter puts it, external apprehended objects belong to the Sautrāntika system, and the proponents of cognition-only have no account of apprehended objects. Sodargye ranks the positions: Sautrāntika as less definitive, Chittamatra as provisionally definitive, Madhyamaka as fully definitive.

## Chapter Two: Examining Cognition

*Tibetan and English: [chapter 2 in the Pramāṇa Wiki](https://pramana.middlewaymusings.com/texts/pramanayuktanidhi/chapter-2-cognition).*

The definition of mind[^c2-1] is awareness.[^c2-2]

By way of object and so forth, it becomes many;  
by way of cognition, it is one self-awareness[^c2-3].[^c2-4]

[The early Tibetan logicians hold:][^c2-5] "Correct assumption[^c2-6],  
appearance-without-ascertainment, subsequent cognition,  
wrong cognition and doubt are the five [invalid cognitions]."

If correct assumption does not depend on a reason at all,  
it is a mere thesis and becomes doubt.  
If it depends on a reason,  
it does not go beyond a valid [reason] or the three invalid ones.[^c2-7]

If the two [kinds of] correct assumption that have a reason  
are not included in the three invalid [reasons],  
there would be a fourth invalid reason.  
If correct assumption is not [among the] invalid [reasons],  
a third valid cognition[^c2-8] would be possible.  
If it is not valid cognition even though it ascertains the truth,  
then inference too would not be [valid cognition].  
If, though it ascertains,[^c2-9] it does not cut off superimposition,  
then [superimposition and certainty]  
would not be the refuted and the refuter.  
If you [Tibetan logicians][^c2-10] say,  
"Even without ascertainment, the three minds  
that are true, false and wavering are not contradictory",  
then without ascertainment correct assumption is lost,  
and if [the wavering] becomes two,  
doubt is hard [to maintain as a separate category].

Or else: just as there is true correct assumption,  
why should wrong correct assumption not be tenable?  
If you accept it, correct assumption's being true is lost;  
if not, the former is equally untenable.  
If it suspects both [alternatives], it becomes doubt.  
If it does exist, the definitive enumeration collapses.  
If it is wrong cognition,  
correct assumption too would be valid cognition,  
since the two are parallel.[^c2-11]

If correct assumption without a reason is true,  
everything would be true.[^c2-12]

If what has a wrong reason is correct assumption,  
this contradicts the treatises.[^c2-13]

What is realised from the scriptures of our own and others' schools  
is included in the four classes of mind;  
it is not correct assumption.[^c2-14]

If appearance-without-ascertainment is not valid cognition,  
then all direct perception would fail to be valid cognition,  
since ascertainment itself has been ruled out for direct perception.[^c2-15]

If subsequent cognition is settled by reasoning,  
it becomes non-realisation, wrong conception,  
or else valid cognition itself;  
a mind other than these is not possible.[^c2-16]

Direct perception is free from conceptual construction  
and [apprehends] in the present;  
subsequent cognition is memory of the past.  
Where object, time and mode of apprehension are contradictory,  
how could there be a common locus?[^c2-17]

You [Tibetan logicians][^c2-18] may say,  
"The apprehension of the continuum of similar type[^c2-19] is direct perception,  
and the ensuing cognition is subsequent cognition";  
[however,] a class is not an object of direct perception,  
and regarding an object seen there is no ensuing cognition.

Because they are contradictory, there is no common locus.  
Because it is direct perception, it is not memory.  
A class is contradictory as the object of a sense faculty.  
Between earlier and later seeing there is no difference.[^c2-20]  
If there is ascertainment, it is not direct perception.  
Apprehending [things] as similar is conceptual cognition.  
Therefore those who know reasoning have refuted  
a common locus of direct perception and subsequent cognition.  
*This is a summary verse.*

The experiencing [sense] consciousness produced  
by however many moments of the gathering  
of the subtle atoms of object and sense faculty:  
that is the direct perception of ordinary beings.[^c2-21]

However many divisions and groupings by number there are,  
they are made by division into classes;  
however many internal divisions and groupings there are,  
they are held to fall within those [classes].[^c2-22]  
*This is an interstitial verse.*

What is not established as non-deceptive is invalid cognition.[^c2-23]

Non-realisation, wrong conception and doubt  
are the three opposites of valid cognition.  
They are divided by way of the mode of engagement;  
gathered by way of their nature, they are one.[^c2-24]

What is empty of apprehending [its object]  
as that or as not that is non-realisation.  
Divided, it is threefold: not engaged, not completed,  
and completed yet not found.[^c2-25]

What apprehends [its object] as that,  
and is refuted by [the apprehension of it as] not that, is wrong cognition.  
Divided, it is twofold, conceptual and non-conceptual;  
divided separately, it is fivefold.[^c2-26]

What apprehends [its object] as that,  
while [admitting] the possibility of it being otherwise, is doubt.  
Divided, it is twofold: manifest and dormant;  
[as well as] evenly balanced and apprehending [one side] more predominantly.[^c2-27]

The two valid cognitions  
are included in self-awareness valid cognition alone;  
so too, the three invalid cognitions  
are nothing other than non-realisation.[^c2-28]  
*This is a summary verse.*

From the *Treasury of Reasoning on Valid Cognition*, the second chapter: the examination of mind.

[^c2-1]: Sanskrit *buddhi*.

[^c2-2]: The auto-commentary rejects the definition of mind as what knows an object. Chapter 1 defined the object as what is cognisable by the mind, so each would depend on the other and neither could be identified; and self-awareness, which has no object other than itself, would not count as cognition. Lowo Khenchen names Tsangnakpa and others as holding that definition.

[^c2-3]: Sanskrit *svasaṃvedana* or *svasaṃvitti*.

[^c2-4]: The auto-commentary spells out "and so forth": by object, mind is conceptual or non-conceptual; by the nature of cognition, mistaken or non-mistaken; by mode of engagement, as with cutting wood, it has not engaged its object, is engaging it, or has engaged it. Gathered from the side of the subject, all of these are included in self-awareness.

[^c2-5]: The auto-commentary names them "most Tibetan scholars, Cha-ba and others"; Sodargye adds Tsangnakpa. Lowo Khenchen traces the wording of the opponents' definitions in this chapter to Tsurtön Zhönnu Sengge's *Lamp of Wisdom on Valid Cognition* (ཚད་མ་ཤེས་རབ་སྒྲོན་མེ་). Sakya Paṇḍita accepts the last two of the five and refutes the first three.

[^c2-6]: Sanskrit *manaḥparīkṣa*.

[^c2-7]: The opponents define correct assumption as an awareness that, without depending on a reason, ascertains a previously unrealised concealed object in accordance with the fact. They divide it into three: without a reason (thinking there is water in one's forefathers' old well); with an inverted reason (concluding there is fire from the flies gathered round the smell of burnt meat); and with an unascertained reason (realising that sound is impermanent from the reason 'produced' without knowing the three-fold criteria) (auto-commentary). The "two [kinds] that have a reason" below are the second and third.

[^c2-8]: Sanskrit *pramāṇa*.

[^c2-9]: The Kabum text reads དེས་ཀྱང་ ("even by that"). Jamtsho's critical edition reads ངེས་ཀྱང་ ("though it ascertains"), as does the auto-commentary, and the translation follows it. The auto-commentary cites the *Pramāṇavārttika*: with regard to one object, the two minds of certainty and superimposition are by nature refuted and refuter.

[^c2-10]: Sodargye identifies the speakers as the earlier Tibetan tradition. Their example in the auto-commentary: thinking 'Is there water in the well?' is correct assumption, thinking 'Is there none?' is wrong cognition, and holding both is doubt. The reply: if the one wavering is split between correct assumption and wrong cognition, no doubt can be found.

[^c2-11]: Wrong correct assumption would ascertain a concealed object not in accordance with the fact. If it is admitted, it is a sixth invalid cognition, and the five are no longer a definitive enumeration (auto-commentary).

[^c2-12]: The auto-commentary: then every conjecture, such as guessing that there is a treasure of jewels under one's house, would be true. Nor is the old well a case without a reason: having once seen water there serves as one, and since that reason is uncertain, [the cognition] is doubt. Sodargye reports Gorampa making the same point.

[^c2-13]: On this view every uncertain reason (proving sound impermanent because it is audible, or because it is an object of comprehension) would yield correct assumption, yet the master classes such cognitions as doubt; and so would every non-established reason (sound is impermanent because it is visible), yet the treatises say a non-established reason produces no realisation (auto-commentary).

[^c2-14]: This answers the view that the wisdom arising from hearing scripture is correct assumption. In the auto-commentary, scripture backed by proof, such as the teaching of the four truths and the two kinds of selflessness, gives valid cognition; scripture with a refuter, such as the claim that bathing and austerities cleanse wrongdoing, gives wrong cognition; scripture with neither proof nor refuter gives doubt where its basis is established and non-realisation where it is not.

[^c2-15]: The opponents define appearance-without-ascertainment as the appearing of a specifically characterised object, such as blue, that does not contradict the superimposition 'not blue'; their three kinds are where the object's aspect is unclear, where the mind is not directed to it, and where a cause of error intervenes (auto-commentary). Sakya Paṇḍita's point, supported in the auto-commentary by a commentary on the *Pramāṇavārttika*, is that direct perception never holds its object by ascertaining it, only by its appearing.

[^c2-16]: The auto-commentary: subsequent cognition is non-realisation, since the object of valid cognition ceases moment by moment and, even had it not, the mind has already realised it. Taking what is realised now to be the very thing realised before is wrong cognition, since it holds as that what is not that. Inferring past experience from present memory is the Yogācāras' proof of the result.

[^c2-17]: The target is the opponents' "perceptual subsequent cognition", such as seeing the same blue just after seeing blue (auto-commentary). The auto-commentary sets out the contrast: direct perception is free from conceptual construction in nature, has the specifically characterised as its object and the present as its time, and apprehends clearly and unmixed; subsequent cognition is conceptual, has the word-object as its object and the past as its time, and apprehends unclearly and mixed. Answering a later objection by the Karmapa Chödrak Gyatso, Sodargye notes that the analysis of objects here stands on the Sautrāntika position.

[^c2-18]: Sodargye identifies the speakers as the earlier Tibetan tradition. The auto-commentary asks what the class is: a string of moments of blue, which is a substance and not a class; the universal of the non-Buddhists, which is impossible; or an elimination of other, which is superimposed and not an entity. None of these is an object of the senses. It takes the ensuing cognition to be certainty or memory, both conceptual.

[^c2-19]: Sanskrit *jātisantāna*.

[^c2-20]: The first blue and the later blue are alike in being a previously unseen specifically characterised object seen by an unmistaken cognition, so either both are valid cognition or neither is (auto-commentary).

[^c2-21]: This answers the objection that if direct perception is confined to the substance of a single moment, ordinary beings could have no valid direct perception (auto-commentary). Their direct perception does not engage individual atoms: it is the consciousness produced over however many moments by an object made of gathered atoms, a sense faculty, mental engagement and so on.

[^c2-22]: Aimed at the fivefold list. Setting up separate classes of mind on the strength of some minor feature would undo every definitive enumeration in Dharmakīrti's system, of valid and invalid cognition and of invalid reasons, as if adventitious inference were not included in inference (auto-commentary).

[^c2-23]: The auto-commentary defends the negative form: just as 'not an ox' is marked out by ruling out the hump and dewlap, there is no fault in defining it this way.

[^c2-24]: Sakya Paṇḍita's own position. The auto-commentary matches the three to the three faults of a reason: a non-established reason has no power to produce realisation, and non-realisation engages neither rightly nor wrongly; an uncertain reason produces wavering, as doubt does; a contradictory reason proves the unwanted, as wrong conception realises what is not so. Stated reasons are only four, one of them faultless, and in the same way a mind's modes of engagement are only four, one of them valid; so invalid cognition is definitively three. By nature, wrong conception and doubt are themselves non-realisation.

[^c2-25]: The auto-commentary's examples: not engaged, as with one who knows no logic and has never turned to the three-fold criteria; not completed, as with one who, asked whether sound is permanent, has begun to examine it; completed yet not found, in three ways: where there is no object to realise, as with subsequent cognition; where the realising mind is corrupted, as with momentariness, not realised from the side of elimination of other; and where it is not an object of knowledge, as with things remote in place, time or nature.

[^c2-26]: The five (auto-commentary). Non-conceptual: a corrupted sense consciousness, as when seeing two moons, and a corrupted mental consciousness, as in a dream. Conceptual: error about aspect, as with a rope taken for a snake; about time, as when thinking the present blue is the past blue, which is what gets counted as subsequent cognition; and about the object, as when thinking this śiṃśapā and sandalwood share one universal.

[^c2-27]: Manifest doubt is actually engaged, as when wondering whether a fire offering wins a higher rebirth or not; dormant doubt is like a seed in a box, with potency once it meets conditions. The auto-commentary places under it every uncertain reason and correct assumption: in short, a mind that engages a concealed object without depending on a reason is, on examination, doubt, whether its content is true or false. Sodargye takes the second pair as a division of manifest doubt.

[^c2-28]: Under the enumeration verse above, the auto-commentary says that both valid cognitions are included in self-awareness direct perception, just as both objects are included in the specifically characterised object of comprehension. Śākya Chokden gives the reason: the direct object of comprehension of both valid cognitions is cognition itself.

## Chapter Three: Examining Universals and Particulars

*Tibetan and English: [chapter 3 in the Pramāṇa Wiki](https://pramana.middlewaymusings.com/texts/pramanayuktanidhi/chapter-3-universals-and-particulars).*

What apprehends the specifically characterised phenomenon is non-conceptual;  
what apprehends the universal[^c3-1] is conceptual cognition.  
Of these, the specifically characterised phenomenon is an entity;  
the universal is not established as an entity.[^c3-2]

Substance and distinguisher, universal and particular,  
affirmation and elimination of other[^c3-3], conceptual and non-conceptual:  
others explain the system otherwise; I intend to explain it  
according to the treatises [of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti].[^c3-4]

Since inference does not apprehend [its object] as an entity,  
a substantial phenomenon is not tenable for it.  
Because inference is exclusion[^c3-5],  
[its object] is held to be established[^c3-6] by way of the distinguisher.[^c3-7]

What is distinguished from the entities  
of other [classes] and of its own [class]  
is [respectively] the universal and the particular.[^c3-8]

There are two [kinds] each of universal and particular:  
the horizontal and the vertical.  
Horizontal particulars are different substances;  
for vertical particulars, being one is negated.[^c3-9]

"[Universal and particular] are different", some say.[^c3-10]

[However,] being different is excluded  
by the absence of what should appear.[^c3-11]

"[They] are one substance", some say.[^c3-12]

[However,] if they are one substance,  
[the universal] should appear but does not.[^c3-13]

If particulars contradictory in place, time and aspect  
are related to one universal,  
their arising and disintegration would become one in nature.  
If not, it is contradictory for one [substance] to have two parts.[^c3-14]

If universal and particular are one substance,  
how then could the whole be refuted?[^c3-16]

It would follow that even [consciousness  
based on] the sense faculty is conceptual.[^c3-17]

All contradictory [things] too would become one substance.[^c3-18]

If you [early Tibetan logicians][^c3-19] say,  
"There are also many universals, related to each particular",

then the definition [of the universal] would not be established,  
and the system of negation and affirmation would collapse too.[^c3-20]

"A single universal of similar definition  
and the particulars are one substance",  
so [the Tibetan logicians][^c3-21] say.

[However,] similarity is joined together by conceptual cognition,  
and a mental phenomenon of conceptual cognition  
does not exist in the object.  
If resemblance existed in the object,  
then even on seeing what had not [been seen] before,  
[it] would resemble [others].[^c3-22]

For what is similar, being one entity is impossible,  
and for what is not one, the word 'universal' does not [apply].  
If a single universal has not appeared to the mind,  
then even if [things] are conceived[^c3-23] as similar,  
it is not fit to be a universal.[^c3-24]

Partial resemblance exists in all [things];  
complete resemblance exists nowhere.[^c3-25]

[These views] also appear[^c3-26] to contradict  
the system taught by the King of Reasoning.[^c3-27]

What is distinguished from the nature of what is not that,  
and is established for all entities,  
is the universal of exclusion;  
on that, mistaken for a specifically characterised phenomenon,  
negation and affirmation are performed.[^c3-28]

If you say, "If there is no universal in the object,  
then, though elimination of other, a superimposition,  
is established as a non-entity,  
it is unrelated to the object,  
and so all conventions collapse":[^c3-29]

Having mistaken the object and exclusion  
as one and the same, one engages the object;  
and since the mere attainment of the specifically characterised phenomenon  
is valid cognition, [this] is tenable.[^c3-31]

The Ulūka School holds the universal to be different;  
"It is one substance", say the Sāṃkhyas;  
those of the Land of Snow follow that [position];  
holding it to be exclusion is the system of the wise.[^c3-32]  
*This is a summary verse.*

From the *Treasury of Reasoning on Valid Cognition*, the third chapter: the examination of universal and particular.

[^c3-1]: Sanskrit *sāmānya*.

[^c3-2]: The auto-commentary sorts the chapter's terms into two sets of synonyms. Specifically characterised phenomenon, instance (གསལ་བ་), entity, substance, the distinguished (ལོག་པ་) and the ultimate name what can perform a function, stays unmixed with other substances, stands as cause and effect, and is what every person engages when taking up and setting aside. Universal, generally characterised phenomenon, elimination of other, distinguisher, preclusion, the mixed, relationship and the relative name what does not abide in the object, is not established as an entity, is not an object of the senses, is superimposed by conceptual cognition, and cannot perform a function.

[^c3-3]: Sanskrit *anyāpoha*.

[^c3-4]: The auto-commentary names the others as the Tibetans: sometimes they explain substance and affirmation, universal and distinguisher, as alike, sometimes as not; sometimes as phenomena of the mind, sometimes of the object; sometimes they prove the universal, sometimes refute it, so that "I have not seen their thinking reach the bottom". Śākya Chokden counts Tsangnakpa among them. Sakya Paṇḍita's own allocation: substance and universal are the objects, the conceptual and the non-conceptual the minds, and appearance and exclusion the modes of apprehension.

[^c3-5]: Sanskrit: inference, *anumāna*; exclusion, *apoha*.

[^c3-6]: Jamtsho's critical edition reads གྲུབ་ ("established") with a single witness, as does the Kabum text. The other witnesses and the auto-commentary have སྒྲུབ་ or a variant spelling of it ("prove"), which would give "inference is held to prove [its object] by way of the distinguisher".

[^c3-7]: The auto-commentary first refutes the Tibetans' definitions: substance is many distinguishers gathered inseparably on one basis, and a distinguisher is each gathered property taken separately, "like a coat of mail and its plates". But distinguishers are superimposed by the mind and do not abide in the object, so there is nothing there to gather; if they did gather, this would be no different from the non-Buddhists' many parts gathered inseparably in a single whole; and each definition depends on the other. Jamtsho finds a very similar passage, coat of mail included, in Tsurtön Zhönnu Sengge's *Lamp of Wisdom on Valid Cognition*, which Sakya Paṇḍita is probably quoting or paraphrasing. The verse then refutes the substantial phenomenon: if inference measured substantial phenomena, then, since produced and impermanent are not different substances in a vase, realising that it is produced would be realising that it is impermanent.

[^c3-8]: Sakya Paṇḍita's own definitions. In the auto-commentary, the universal is a superimposition appearing as a single distinguisher, turned away from the dissimilar class: to a conceptual cognition apprehending a vase, the instances of vase appear as turned away from non-vase. The particular is that same [superimposition], turned away also from within the similar class and appearing as two distinguishers together: to a conceptual cognition apprehending a tree, what appears as a śiṃśapā. Both are superimpositions. Jamtsho finds a very similar definition in Tsurtön's *Lamp of Wisdom*.

[^c3-9]: The auto-commentary: śiṃśapā, sandalwood and the like, different substances gathered by direction, are horizontal particulars, and the universal related to them is the horizontal universal; the earlier and later moments of a single entity such as a śiṃśapā, gathered by time, are vertical particulars, and the universal related to them is the vertical universal. Horizontal particulars depend on direction, so they are different substances. Vertical particulars depend on time, and since the present is an entity while past and future are not, only their being one is negated. Śākya Chokden, approving Terawa (known as the new Dignāga), says the pair is not Sakya Paṇḍita's own system, since the terms occur nowhere in the works of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti: it is a Naiyāyika view reported in the *Ornament of Valid Cognition* (ཚད་མ་རྒྱན་), set out here in passing. Terawa himself says the Vaiśeṣikas coined the two terms. The image is literal. ཐད་ཀ is "level, side by side": things that exist together in different places lie along one line. གོང་མ is "upper", and in Tibetan what is above is also what is earlier, so the moments of one thing are stacked one on another. Jamtsho gives the Sanskrit as *tiryaksāmānya* and *ūrdhvatā*, and Monier-Williams glosses *tiryañc* "horizontal (opposed to *ūrdhva*)". Sodargye's Chinese has 异体 "distinct-substance" and 先后 "successive", glosses drawn from the commentary.

[^c3-10]: The auto-commentary identifies them as the non-Buddhist Vaiśeṣikas. Their great universal, existence, and limited universals such as cowness pervade all their instances and are different substances from them: permanent, partless and non-appearing, and joined to the instances by the relationship of inherence, "like a herd of oxen tied to one rope".

[^c3-11]: The reasoning is the nonapprehension of that which should appear. The auto-commentary: a universal other than instances such as [particular] trees is not established as an entity, since it is not observed although it should appear, like a rabbit's horns; to be certain that something exists even though it could not appear would go much too far.

[^c3-12]: The auto-commentary names two parties. The Sāṃkhyas hold that the universal, primal substance (the three guṇas in equal balance), is present in particulars such as a vase as a single self, like fire and its heat. The Tibetans proclaim with one voice that a "mere tree" over śiṃśapā, sandalwood and the like, a "mere blue" over beryl and other blues, and a "mere ox" are one with their particulars, indivisible in substance and nature: "the Sāṃkhyas who hold this and the Tibetans agree in meaning". Śākya Chokden says that from Cha-ba and Tsangnakpa to his own day all have agreed on this. The auto-commentary divides the one-substance view into three: one universal one with many particulars (refuted in the next five verses), many universals one with many particulars, and a universal of similarity.

[^c3-13]: Since universal and particular are one substance, when the particular appears, the universal, indivisible from it, should appear too; its nonapprehension refutes it (auto-commentary).

[^c3-14]: The auto-commentary: when one tree arises or perishes, if the tree-universal does not arise or perish, they are not one substance; if it does, trees of different places would arise in one place, trees of different times at one time and trees of different aspects in one aspect, and when one perished all would perish. It cites the *Pramāṇaviniścaya*: things differ only by having contradictory properties or different causes, and if that does not make them different, nothing differs from anything and everything becomes a single substance.

[^c3-16]: The arguments by which [Buddhists] refute a single whole pervading head, legs and the other parts (that what is related to many is not one; that when the hand moved the whole body would move; and those from being covered or uncovered, dyed or undyed) apply equally to a single universal (auto-commentary). Sodargye notes that the charge bites only on Buddhists, since a Sāṃkhya never refuted the whole: its target is the Tibetans.

[^c3-17]: A single substance has no part that appears and part that does not, so when the particular appears to a sense consciousness, the universal appears too, and that consciousness becomes conceptual (auto-commentary). To the reply that apprehending a conceptual generality is conceptual but apprehending a type-universal is not, the auto-commentary answers that to hold a type-universal not included in the conceptual generality is like holding an ocean that is not water: "the babble of one drunk on a bad teacher's poison-water".

[^c3-18]: Different things such as white and blue, and contradictories such as light and darkness, would all be one substance. Then wanting blue one would get white, wanting darkness light, wanting the universal the particular, and everything would fall into confusion (auto-commentary).

[^c3-19]: The auto-commentary says only "some", who grant that there is no single universal but hold that on each particular, such as a śiṃśapā, there is a universal one in nature with it. Rongtön attributes the view to "some Tibetans" (བོད་འགའ་ཞིག་).

[^c3-20]: If there are many universals, the universal of śiṃśapā is a collection of two distinguishers, which makes it a particular, and the definition of the universal is not established. And establishing the particular śiṃśapā would not establish the universal of other trees, nor would excluding the universal of śiṃśapā exclude other trees, so the system of negation and affirmation collapses (auto-commentary).

[^c3-21]: In the auto-commentary, "some" accept neither one universal nor many, but hold that the universal in which śiṃśapā, sandalwood and the like are similar in definition, having branches and leaves, is one substance with the particulars. Śākya Chokden attributes the view to Tsangnakpa Tsöndrü Sengge; Sodargye, to the early Tibetan school of pramāṇa.

[^c3-22]: Putting resemblances together is conceptual cognition, so resemblance does not exist in the object. If it did, on seeing a ruby never seen before one would have to think that it resembles other rubies (auto-commentary). The auto-commentary cites the *Pramāṇavārttika*: those who apprehend resemblance are those who have seen both what resembles and what it resembles.

[^c3-23]: The Kabum text and the auto-commentary read རྟོགས་ ("realised"). Jamtsho's critical edition reads རྟོག་ ("conceived") and records no variant; the translation follows it.

[^c3-24]: The auto-commentary first reports a fallback: "Even if there is no similar universal in the object, why should there not be a universal that conceptual cognition apprehends as similar?" Śākya Chokden attributes it to Denbakpa Mawé Sengge, a disciple of Cha-ba. The answer: the word "similar" already indicates many phenomena, since a single phenomenon cannot be similar to that same one. To the further claim that apprehending as similar is itself the universal, even if nothing appears as one, the auto-commentary replies that one could no longer say "bring wood", only "bring something like śiṃśapā, bring something like sandalwood".

[^c3-25]: Because of differences of place, time and aspect, resemblance in every respect is nowhere possible. So pervasion too is not established through the resemblance of the smoke on the mountain pass and the smoke in the kitchen, but by conceiving them as one universal through elimination of other (auto-commentary).

[^c3-26]: Jamtsho's critical edition adopts འགྱུར་ ("would contradict") from one witness. The Kabum text, the auto-commentary and his other witnesses read སྣང་ ("appear"), which the translation keeps.

[^c3-27]: The auto-commentary answers from Dharmakīrti, quoting the *Pramāṇavārttika*: the mind of a universal is corrupted by a cause of error; the universal that is the word-object is imputed by conceptual cognition; the universal of impermanence and the like is a non-entity, and so not the sphere of the senses. Sodargye takes the King of Reasoning to be Dignāga and Dharmakīrti together.

[^c3-28]: Sakya Paṇḍita's own position. Conceptual cognition, mistaken by nature, takes the universal and particular of elimination of other for the universal and particular of the object; on that [mistake] rests the system in which establishing the particular establishes the universal and excluding the universal excludes the particular (auto-commentary).

[^c3-29]: The auto-commentary spells out what would collapse: the reasoning of identical nature, by which establishing the particular establishes the universal; the nonapprehension of the pervader, by which excluding the universal excludes the particular; and with them every logical system and all worldly activity.

[^c3-31]: Conceptual cognition is mistaken by nature, so when it engages [its object] having mistaken elimination of other for the specifically characterised phenomenon, the mere attainment of the object accomplishes what valid cognition does. "Attainment" is just this: knowing, when one proceeds according to the negation or affirmation made, whether the object exists or not. Every other dispute resolves itself once elimination of other is understood, so none is stated here (auto-commentary).

[^c3-32]: The Ulūka School is the Vaiśeṣika, named after its founder's epithet Ulūka, "owl". Those of the Land of Snow are Cha-ba and his successors (Sodargye). Gorampa explains why they count as followers of the Sāṃkhyas: they do not say outright that the universal is an entity, but accept that it is one substance with entities. He also records Sakya Paṇḍita's quip that those of the Land of Snow (གངས་ཅན་པ་) agree with the Sāṃkhyas (གྲངས་ཅན་), and the Sakyapa is at odds with Kapila (སེར་སྐྱ་).
