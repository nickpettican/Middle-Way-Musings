## On being Triyana

It's not surprising that we need so many years of study in the Tibetan tradition once you realise that we are actually Triyana practitioners. Triyana means that we follow all three yanas, or vehicles. The first, the Sravakayana, is a must, since it is the base and the foundation upon which the other two vehicles depend. This is especially important for monastics, because the Vinaya, the monastic code of conduct, belongs entirely to the Sravaka path. None of the elements that define the Mahayana sutras appear in the surviving Vinaya scriptures. There is no bodhisattva vow, no generating of Bodhichitta, and no training in the perfections. Every rule in the Vinaya can be kept in full by a Sravakayana follower; one does not need to be Mahayana at all.[^1] And while Tibetans and East Asian Mahayana traditions might refer to their Vinaya as "Mahayana", I invite you to try and find anything Mahayana within their Vinaya Pitaka.

The second yana is the Mahayana, the path of the Bodhisattva, also called the Samyaksambodhiyana, the path to complete and perfect Buddhahood. Those who follow this path either replace or supplement the Sutra and Abhidharma Pitakas with the Mahayana sutras and shastras. And those who also practise the Secret Mantra vehicle, the Vajrayana, add a fourth Pitaka. Yijing called it the Vidyadhara Pitaka,[^2] and nowadays we refer to it as the Buddhist Tantras.

Each of these yanas comes with its own vows, commitments and trainings, some of which actually clash with and contradict each other. For this reason the Buddha gave teachings such as the *Sutra Determining the Vinaya: Upali's Questions*, to explain how one must practise when following more than one yana.[^3] Later, masters such as Sakya Pandita gathered the scattered instructions into a single text, the *Clear Differentiation of the Three Vows*, to clarify how one must practise the three layers of vows together.[^4]

In short, each yana builds upon the previous one, and each higher yana protects the vows of the one beneath it. The Buddha explained to Upali that if one holds Bodhichitta and commits an action that is seen as a fault on the Sravaka path, the action will not become a fault as long as it is motivated by Bodhichitta. Even so, a "fault" here means a minor infraction. It therefore most likely does not include an act of defeat, such as breaking one of the four root vows of a monastic.

## The fourth pitaka

Some Vajrayana practices include playing music or taking a fermented drink, both of which are faults in the Sravakayana. However, since Vajrayana is a higher practice, it essentially protects the Sravaka practice from incurring a fault. Unfortunately, this also opens the door to convenience for practitioners who are not genuine.

Take meat. In the Sravakayana it is permissible to eat meat as long as it meets the criteria set by the Buddha. In the Mahayana, however, it is generally not permissible to eat meat, since it damages the Bodhichitta vow.[^5] Even so, those who practise Vajrayana seem to deem it acceptable, given that tantric practice transforms any worldly activity into a meritorious one, even one that the other yanas see as non-virtuous. But how many practitioners are actually doing their due diligence, and how many are just doing it out of convenience? After all, meat is very tasty. But how many modern tantrikas would be able to eat the rotting flesh of a dead animal?

Similarly, these days it is becoming increasingly difficult to find a Triyana monastic who is concerned about keeping any monastic vow other than the four root vows and the thirteen secondary ones. When asked why, the response is either "we are Mahayana" or "modern times are different". However, the monks of the Theravada Forest Tradition have managed to keep almost every vow, even in the modern Western world. Given that, this reasoning loses its validity and starts to smell like convenience.

It's not the case that Tibetan monasteries are not interested in discipline and rules. In some cases, though, the rules are followed only as long as they are convenient. The vast majority serve dinner at night, allow monastics to own money, and overlook all the minor vows, because they consider themselves Mahayana, or Vajrayana, depending on who you ask. Interestingly enough, the Mahayana vows are not strictly enforced either. In fact, sometimes minor rules are placed above the higher practice. Out of convenience.

It should be known that to receive the Vidyadhara Pitaka along with its empowerments, one must first have Bodhichitta. The higher vehicles are not a way around the lower one; they are built on top of it. If the foundation is missing, so is the building.

## An old problem

None of this is new. Tibet has been here before, and the cure it found last time was, quite literally, a Vinaya cure.

In the tenth century, Lha Lama Yeshe Ö, a king of Guge and Purang in Western Tibet, took monastic vows in 989\. One of his writings survives: an ordinance in which he forbids certain tantric practices.[^6] The targets were tantric practitioners in the villages who took the tantric language of "union" and "liberation" literally. They pursued these practices for selfish or harmful ends, without the Mahayana motivation that is supposed to stand behind them. Yeshe Ö also sent young Tibetans, among them the future translator Rinchen Zangpo, to Kashmir to find out what authentic practice actually looked like.[^7]

His successors went further and invited the great Bengali master Atisha, who arrived in Western Tibet in 1042\. When Jangchub Ö put his questions to him, Atisha answered with the *Lamp for the Path to Awakening*. Two of its points are worth remembering here.

First, Atisha teaches that only those who maintain one of the seven kinds of Pratimoksha vows have the proper fortune for the bodhisattva vow.[^8] The Sravaka foundation is not optional.

Second, he states that the secret and wisdom empowerments should not be taken by those who keep celibacy, because this is strongly prohibited in the tantras themselves.[^9]

Atisha was no enemy of the Vajrayana; he was one of its great masters. What he refused to do was let the Vajrayana become an excuse. He used the Vinaya to protect the Vajrayana from its own counterfeits, and the Vajrayana to give meaning to the Vinaya. A thousand years later, we seem to need the same medicine.

## The test

If a higher vow can protect a lower one, how do we tell a genuine exception from a convenient one? Asanga answers this precisely in the Chapter on Ethics of the Bodhisattvabhumi, a principal basis of the bodhisattva vow as it is practised in Tibet.

Asanga divides the rules the Buddha laid down for Sravakas into two kinds.

The first kind are rules made to protect the faith of those who do not yet have faith in the Dharma, and to increase the faith of those who already have it. In these, says Asanga, the bodhisattva trains exactly as the Sravaka does, and no less.

The second kind are rules made so that the Sravaka can have few aims, few concerns and few activities, devoted to his own liberation. Here the bodhisattva does not train in the same way, because his aim is the welfare of others. Asanga's example is that a bodhisattva may accept many robes and bowls from donors, which a Sravaka would refuse, provided he accepts them for the sake of others.

Asanga goes further. He allows a bodhisattva, out of compassion, to commit certain actions that are normally non-virtuous. But he explicitly states that sexual conduct is never permitted to a renunciant bodhisattva, because he must protect the Sravaka teaching from being undermined. Neglecting the first kind of rule is itself one of the bodhisattva's secondary faults: failing to act according to one's vowed training in ways that would generate or sustain the faith of others.

This gives us a clear test, and it is a simple one: who benefits? If an exception serves beings, it is Mahayana. If it serves me, my comfort, my dinner, my wallet, it is not Mahayana. It is convenience wearing a Mahayana mask.

So we can ask the honest question. Does the evening meal benefit any being? Does handling money serve beings, or does it serve me? Asanga does allow a bodhisattva to accept gold and silver, but as a rule of the second kind, and only for the sake of others. In our own Mulasarvastivada Vinaya, eating at the wrong time is a patayantika, and taking hold of gold and silver is a patayantika requiring forfeiture. These are not obscure rules. They are among the most visible signs of renunciation that a layperson sees. If any rule belongs to Asanga's first category, the evening meal does; and money that serves only me has no place in his second.

The *Upali's Questions* points in the same direction. There the Buddha teaches that the pure ethical discipline of the Sravakas is impure for bodhisattvas, and that for a bodhisattva, faults born of desire are lighter than faults born of anger, because anger forsakes beings while desire brings beings together.[^10] These are powerful teachings, and for exactly that reason they are easily abused. They do not say that bodhisattva ethics is looser than Sravaka ethics. They say it is measured by a different standard, the standard of others' welfare, and that standard is harder to meet, not easier. "Desire brings beings together" is about the bodhisattva who refuses to abandon beings. It is not about the monk who refuses to abandon dinner.

## Meat, again

The case of meat shows the pattern clearly. Geoffrey Barstow's study of vegetarianism in Tibet points out the tension at the heart of the matter. Tibetan Buddhism teaches compassion for all beings, animals explicitly included, yet historically most Tibetans, monastic and lay, made meat a regular part of their diet. Barstow argues that religious perspectives largely supported vegetarianism, while practical concerns and secular ideals pulled in the other direction.[^11]

What may surprise many practitioners is that some of the strongest critiques of everyday meat eating in Tibet came from within the tantric tradition itself. The samaya commitments do call for meat and alcohol in the tantric feast, as a way of transcending notions of pure and impure.[^12] But a ritual substance in a feast offering is one thing; a plate of momos at lunch is another. Masters such as Shabkar Tsogdruk Rangdrol made exactly this point and gave up meat themselves.[^13] Barstow also notes that vegetarianism has been spreading in recent decades, adopted above all by monastics.[^14] So the tradition has never lacked the resources to see through the convenience. It has only lacked the will to use them.

## "Modern times are different"

The second favourite argument is that the minor rules belong to another age. But the monks of the Thai Forest Tradition, the lineage of Ajahn Chah, have been living in Britain, Europe, North America and Australia for decades. They eat before midday, they do not handle money, and they go on alms round in English villages. They are not perfect, and Theravada as a whole has its own share of monks who handle money and eat in the evening. But the Forest monasteries prove that the modern world does not make the Vinaya impossible. It only makes it inconvenient.

And it is worth remembering that the rules in question are not peculiar to the Theravada. Their Pali Vinaya and our Mulasarvastivada Vinaya agree on them. When we set them aside, we are not choosing between two traditions. We are choosing between our own Vinaya and our own comfort.

## Convenience in the other direction

There is a second face of convenience, and it is just as damaging. Sometimes it does not appear as laxity but as strictness.

A rule can be enforced not because it protects anyone's faith or anyone's training, but simply because it is convenient for whoever is in charge. Saying "no" is easier than thinking. In these cases, a minor rule, or worse, a mere institutional custom, gets placed above the higher practice. Among Asanga's secondary faults for a bodhisattva are refusing to teach the Dharma to those who ask for it, and failing to help others in what is meaningful.[^15] A monastic culture that is strict about everything that hinders bodhisattva activity, and lax about everything that would cost it comfort, has turned the Three Vows upside down. It is rigid where the bodhisattva should be flexible, and flexible where even the Sravaka would be rigid.

To be fair, rules and good order matter. A monastery is a community, not a collection of individuals, and a monk who invokes Bodhichitta every time he wants to do as he pleases is practising convenience too. But that is exactly the point. The question is never "which yana gives me the answer I want?" The question is always: does this serve beings, does it protect faith, and does it honour every level of vow I hold?

## Not out of convenience

I include myself in all of this. It is easy to see convenience in others and very hard to see it in oneself. Each of us who holds the three sets of vows is constantly tempted to reach for the vow that best suits the moment: the Vinaya when we want to appear disciplined, the Mahayana when we want to be excused, and the Vajrayana when we want to be excused from the excuses.

But the system was never designed for picking and choosing. It was designed as a single building. The Pratimoksha is the foundation, Bodhichitta is the structure, and the Vajrayana is the roof. A higher vow only protects a lower one when it is held with genuine Bodhichitta, and genuine Bodhichitta is never convenient. It always costs us something.

So the next time we hear "we are Mahayana" or "modern times are different", it is worth asking whether we are speaking out of Bodhichitta, or out of convenience.
